So, you have decided to enter the world of bug bounty. The first thing you need is a legal playground. You cannot hunt on random websites. That is trespassing in the digital world. The professional way is to use designated platforms. Think of platforms like HackerOne, Bugcrowd, and Intigriti. These are not just websites; they are the official bug bounty hunting grounds. Companies pay these platforms to host their programs. When you find a vulnerability on these platforms, you report it through their secure system, and if it is valid, you get paid. This is the only safe way to start. It guarantees that the company has given explicit permission for you to test their systems.

But simply signing up is not enough. You need a strategy. The biggest mistake beginners make is jumping straight into scanning without a plan. That is like walking into a locked house with a lockpick set and hoping for the best. You will likely trigger alarms and get banned. Instead, you need to understand the lay of the land. Start by reading the program rules carefully. Every program has a 'Rules of Engagement' document. It tells you what is in bounds and what is out of bounds. It specifies the scope. Scope defines exactly which URLs, domains, and IP addresses you are allowed to test. Testing outside the scope is the fastest way to get your account suspended permanently.

Now, let us talk about the one skill that matters more than tools. Beginners often obsess over buying the latest expensive software or learning complex scripting. But the single most important skill you can develop is 'input testing' or 'probing for edge cases'. Imagine you have a login page. A novice types a password and moves on. A pro does not just type a password; they probe the boundaries. They might type a single quote character, or a massive string of random characters, into the username field. Why? Because they are testing for edge cases. They are seeing how the application handles unexpected input. This is the essence of bug hunting. You are not just using tools; you are thinking like a detective, probing for weaknesses in the logic. If you master input testing, you do not need a dozen tools. You will find vulnerabilities that others miss because they are too reliant on automated scanners.

But wait, there is a catch. Even if you stay within the scope and test inputs, there is one major pitfall that catches everyone off guard. It is not about the code; it is about your behavior. And we will cover that in just a moment.

[TEASE 1 END]

Let us pivot to the legal and ethical side, which is non-negotiable. I must stress: never, ever test a system you do not own or have explicit written permission to test. Some beginners get excited and try to test their friend's website or a random company's API. This is a violation of computer fraud and abuse laws in many jurisdictions. In India and the US, laws like the IT Act and CFAA are strict. The rule of thumb is simple: if you did not sign a contract or see a public bug bounty program listing you as a tester, keep your hands off. Your lab is your best friend. Set up a local environment using VirtualBox or VMware. Install vulnerable applications intentionally, like DVWA (Damn Vulnerable Web App) or OWASP Juice Shop. These are designed for education. You can break them all you want. This builds your muscle memory without any risk to real companies.

Now, let us discuss the technical side of reporting. Once you find a vulnerability, how do you prove it? You cannot just say 'the login is broken.' You need a reproducible proof of concept. This is a step-by-step guide that a developer can follow to recreate the bug. A good report includes the URL, the exact request sent, the parameters modified, and the expected vs. actual behavior. For example, if you find a input validation flaw, you would show exactly how unexpected characters were entered into a parameter and how the application responded. This level of detail is what gets bugs accepted and paid for. Sloppy reports get deleted. Detailed reports get rewards.

And speaking of details, there is a common myth that you need to be a coding genius to succeed. I am going to debunk that right after this short break on how to structure your findings.

A pro tip for your reports is the 'Impact Statement.' Do not just show the bug; explain the risk. If an attacker exploits this, what can they do? Can they steal user sessions? Can they deface the website? Can they gain admin access? Quantify the impact. Saying 'This input validation flaw allows session hijacking' is much more compelling than 'I found a weird error.' Companies prioritize bugs that pose a real threat to their data or reputation. Also, learn to use Markdown or a simple text editor to format your reports. A wall of unformatted text is hard to read. Use bullet points, code blocks for commands, and clear headings. It shows professionalism.
